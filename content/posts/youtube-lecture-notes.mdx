A useful lecture note helps you do something after the video ends. It might help you reconstruct an argument, solve a similar problem or identify the exact point where you became confused. A paragraph copied from the screen does not necessarily do any of those things.

## Decide what you are trying to learn

Before pressing play, write one question. For a lecture about sorting algorithms, it could be: “When would I choose merge sort instead of an in-place sort?” This gives your attention a task. You do not need to preserve every sentence; you need enough evidence to answer the question.

During the first section, listen for the structure. Is the speaker introducing a definition, working through an example or defending a conclusion? Pause at a transition, rather than interrupting every few seconds to transcribe.

## Separate observation from understanding

Use three kinds of notes:

- **What the speaker showed:** a definition, example or diagram.
- **What I think it means:** your explanation in ordinary language.
- **What I still need to check:** the unresolved question.

For example, a screenshot might show an array being divided. Your explanation could be: “Splitting makes smaller problems, but the merge step is where the ordered result is assembled.” The unresolved question might be: “Why is the extra memory needed during merging?”

Those are different jobs. Keeping them separate stops a captured slide from masquerading as your own understanding.

## Save a frame when the visual matters

Capture the diagram or worked example that your explanation depends on. Include a timestamp so you can return to the original context. A timestamp without a meaningful note still leaves future-you wondering why you saved it.

In Clip Insights, use **Snapshot** for the frame and the note composer for your explanation. Keep the notes brief enough to scan, but include the reason a step follows. See [your first study session](/guides/getting-started) for the controls.

## End with a reconstruction

At the end of a section, look away from the video and try to explain the idea. Use the gap in your explanation to decide what to revisit. You do not need to mark the entire lecture as unclear when the real problem is one missing connection.

Then export a PDF and add a specific next action: “Try the same process on a five-item array without the video.” A note becomes more useful when it leads to an attempt.

## Keep the source with the notes

Save the video title and source context with your material. If you share a document, respect the creator's rights and your course's rules. Preserve quotations as quotations, and do not attribute your interpretation to the lecturer.

Download your PDF before clearing local browser data. Uploading a PDF is optional and separate from the editable local timeline; [the export guide](/guides/notes-and-pdf-export) explains the difference.
